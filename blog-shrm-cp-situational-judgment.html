Mastering Situational Judgment Questions on the SHRM-CP

How SHRM-CP situational judgment items work, a five-step method for choosing the best answer, and three practice scenarios with full walkthroughs.

10 min read • September 2026

In this article

  1. What situational judgment items are
  2. A five-step method
  3. Common wrong-answer patterns
  4. Three practice scenarios
  5. How to practice

Situational judgment items (SJIs) are what make the SHRM-CP feel different from a typical certification exam. On the current exam, 54 of the 134 questions are scenario-based. Every option may sound reasonable, and more than one could work in real life. You only get credit for the best one.

What situational judgment items are

SHRM describes SJIs as realistic work scenarios followed by a question asking for the best strategy. Although more than one strategy may be effective, the credited answer is the one chosen by a panel of experienced HR professionals.

That last detail is the key. You're not choosing what your current employer would do, or what's fastest. You're choosing what a panel of experienced HR practitioners would agree is the most effective, ethical, and professionally sound response at an operational level.

A five-step method

  1. Find the actual question. Read the final sentence first. "What should the HR manager do first?" is a different question from "What is the best long-term approach?"
  2. Identify the core issue. Strip the story down to one line: a legal risk, a conflict, a performance problem, a change management issue.
  3. Name the competency. Is it testing Ethical Practice? Communication? Consultation? Knowing this tells you what "good" looks like.
  4. Eliminate with reasons. Cross out anything illegal, unethical, retaliatory, or outside HR's role. Then cross out anything that skips a step.
  5. Choose between the last two. Prefer the option that gathers facts, involves the right stakeholders, addresses root cause, and is consistent with policy.

Common wrong-answer patterns

  • The jump-to-discipline answer. Terminating or disciplining before investigating is almost never best.
  • The do-nothing answer. "Wait and see" or "tell the employee to handle it themselves" usually ignores HR's responsibility.
  • The going-around answer. Bypassing the manager, the policy, or the chain of command without a good reason.
  • The partial answer. Correct, but addresses a symptom instead of the underlying problem.
  • The overreach answer. An operational HR professional unilaterally rewriting company strategy.

Three practice scenarios

These are original practice scenarios, written to reflect the style of SJIs. Try each before reading the walkthrough.

Scenario 1: The complaint about a top performer

An HR generalist at a 300-person distribution company receives an email from a warehouse associate. The associate says their supervisor, the site's highest-performing manager, repeatedly makes jokes about the associate's religion in team huddles. The associate asks HR to keep the complaint confidential and not "make a big deal of it."

What should the HR generalist do first?

  1. Honor the request and take no action unless the associate complains again.
  2. Explain that HR will keep the matter as confidential as possible but must look into it, then begin a prompt, impartial investigation.
  3. Ask the supervisor's manager to have an informal conversation with the supervisor about appropriate humor.
  4. Issue a written warning to the supervisor based on the associate's email.

Best answer: B. The complaint describes possible religious harassment, which the employer has a duty to address once it's on notice. HR can't promise total confidentiality, but it can limit disclosure to people who need to know.

A leaves the company exposed and the employee unprotected. C skips an investigation and informally tips off the accused. D disciplines before gathering facts. The supervisor's performance record is irrelevant, and noticing that is part of what the question tests.

Scenario 2: The turnover spike

A regional clinic group's HR manager notices that voluntary turnover among front-desk staff has nearly doubled over two quarters. The operations director wants to approve an across-the-board $2/hour raise immediately to stop the losses.

What is the best course of action for the HR manager?

  1. Support the raise, since pay is the most common reason people leave.
  2. Recommend reviewing exit interview data, stay interview feedback, and local market pay data before deciding on a solution.
  3. Launch a recognition program to boost morale while the director considers the raise.
  4. Tell the director that compensation decisions are outside the scope of the turnover problem.

Best answer: B. This tests Business Acumen, Consultation, and Analytical Aptitude. The raise could help, but nobody yet knows why people are leaving. Scheduling, a particular supervisor, or workload could be the real cause.

A commits money to an assumption. C is a solution without a diagnosis. D is inaccurate and unhelpful. The best HR response brings evidence to the decision.

Scenario 3: The AI screening tool

A recruiter proposes adopting an AI resume-screening tool to handle high application volume. The vendor says the tool "eliminates bias." The HR manager is asked for a recommendation.

What should the HR manager recommend?

  1. Adopt the tool, since the vendor has addressed bias concerns.
  2. Reject the tool, because AI should not be used in hiring decisions.
  3. Pilot the tool with human review of its recommendations and monitor selection rates across demographic groups for adverse impact.
  4. Let the recruiting team decide, since it's their workflow.

Best answer: C. The 2026 BASK added AI throughout the framework. The employer remains responsible for discriminatory outcomes, regardless of what a vendor claims. A controlled rollout with human oversight and adverse impact monitoring balances efficiency with legal and ethical risk.

A accepts a vendor claim without checking. B is an absolute position that ignores a legitimate business need. D gives up HR's advisory role on a risk issue.

How to practice

  • Work scenarios in sets of 20 to 30, then review every option, not just the correct one.
  • Write one sentence explaining why each wrong option is wrong. After a few weeks, you'll see the same five patterns repeat.
  • Keep your real-world habits in check. Your workplace may do things differently. The exam rewards best practice.
  • Practice under time pressure once your accuracy is stable, since scenario items take the longest to read.
